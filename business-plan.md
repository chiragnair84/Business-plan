# Side Business Plan: Niche Engineering Spares

**Location:** Anand, Gujarat, India  
**Approach:** Build gradually while remaining employed  
**Initial target:** Validate a repeat-purchase product before investing in stock or tooling  
**Target gross margin:** 15–20%, to be confirmed with real quotations and costs

## 1. Objective

Build a small B2B trading and white-label business supplying simple engineering spare parts manufactured by local vendors. Start with one product, sell to reachable distributors and repair businesses, and expand only when repeat orders demonstrate demand.

## 2. Founder constraints

- Continue the current job during validation.
- Use Saturdays and holidays for customer visits.
- Make calls and follow up with prospects on weekdays.
- Operate alone initially; do not hire employees at the discovery stage.
- Prefer simple manufacturing, low tooling costs, and manageable inventory.
- Prioritise repeat consumption and reorders over one-off sales.
- Aim for 15–20% gross margin, but calculate all costs before deciding.

## 3. Initial product hypothesis: pump spare parts

Investigate small wear components used in submersible pumps. Interstage bushes are one candidate, not yet a confirmed opportunity. Do not assume sizes, compatibility, demand, or supplier gaps without customer and supplier evidence.

Validate:
- Which exact part numbers and pump families sell in the highest quantities?
- How often do distributors and repairers reorder them?
- What are current buying prices and resale prices?
- Which suppliers already serve the market, and what problems remain?
- What materials, dimensions, tolerances, and service-life requirements apply?
- Can local vendors manufacture consistently at a viable price?
- What minimum order quantity, inspection, and working capital are required?

## 4. Customer discovery

Speak with pump-spares distributors, repair workshops, service centres, and relevant manufacturers. Start with prospects reachable from Anand and nearby markets.

Ask each prospect:
1. Which pump brands and models do you sell or service most?
2. Which small spare parts do you reorder most often?
3. What are the exact part numbers and monthly quantities?
4. What price do you currently pay?
5. Which sourcing or quality problems do you face?
6. Can you share a sample, drawing, or specification?
7. Would you test a sample from a new supplier? What conditions must be met?

Record the date, contact, evidence, next action, and follow-up date. A paid trial or repeat order is stronger evidence than verbal interest.

## 5. Supplier and quality validation

- Obtain samples, drawings, and specifications before quoting a part as compatible.
- Request written quotations from at least two potential manufacturers where practical.
- Compare material, tolerances, finish, minimum order quantity, lead time, packaging, and payment terms.
- Define incoming inspection checks and retain traceability records.
- Trial samples with customers before committing to a larger batch.
- Track defects, returns, complaints, and corrective actions.
- Check branding, intellectual-property, and applicable regulatory requirements before private-labelling a design.

## 6. Unit economics

**Landed variable cost** = purchase/manufacturing cost + inspection + packaging + inbound freight + other per-unit costs.

**Contribution per unit** = selling price excluding applicable indirect tax − landed variable cost − sales commission − delivery subsidy − expected returns/warranty cost − other variable selling costs.

**Gross margin** = (net selling price − cost of goods sold) ÷ net selling price × 100%.

Do not confuse margin with markup. Include credit periods, inventory holding, returns, and overheads when assessing actual profitability and cash needs.

## 7. 30-day validation plan

### Week 1 — Desk research
- List distributors, repairers, and potential manufacturers.
- Identify candidate part numbers from customer evidence.
- Record current suppliers, pricing, and possible gaps.
- Do not order inventory yet.

### Week 2 — Customer conversations
- Contact prospects by phone during weekdays.
- Visit selected distributors or repairers on Saturday.
- Ask for consumption, part numbers, current prices, samples, and sourcing pain points.
- Rank products by evidence of repeat demand.

### Week 3 — Supplier quotations
- Obtain quotations for the strongest candidate.
- Compare quality capability, minimum order quantity, lead time, and payment terms.
- Calculate landed cost and realistic margins.
- Arrange sample testing only where credible customer interest exists.

### Week 4 — Decision
- Summarise customer and supplier evidence.
- Seek a paid trial order or concrete buying commitment.
- Proceed only if quality can be controlled, cash exposure is affordable, and economics are viable.
- Otherwise, test another candidate without accumulating stock.

## 8. Product scorecard

Score each candidate from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), using evidence where possible.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Repeat purchase frequency and annual consumption | 25% |
| Evidence of unmet need or supplier gap | 20% |
| Achievable margin and price stability | 20% |
| Manufacturing simplicity and quality control | 15% |
| Ease of reaching buyers with available time | 10% |
| Low tooling, inventory, and working-capital needs | 10% |

Use the score as a prioritisation aid, not as proof of demand. Record the source and date for important claims.

## 9. Go/no-go criteria

Start a limited pilot only when:
- Multiple independent buyers confirm the same part and credible repeat consumption.
- Specifications and compatibility are established.
- A supplier can produce consistent parts under a practical quality-control process.
- Real quotations support the margin target after variable costs.
- Initial order quantities and cash exposure are affordable.
- At least one buyer agrees to test or purchase a small batch.

Do not buy machinery, hire staff, or hold broad inventory during initial validation.

## 10. Simple research tracker

Track prospect, buyer type, location, pump model, part number, estimated demand and evidence, current supplier, purchase price, specification, sample status, manufacturer quote, minimum order quantity, lead time, landed cost, selling price, margin, next action, follow-up date, trial orders, reorders, and defects.

## 11. Immediate next actions

1. Contact five pump-spares distributors or repair workshops.
2. Ask each for the three small parts they reorder most frequently.
3. Request part numbers, samples or drawings, monthly quantities, and current buying prices.
4. Contact two local manufacturers after narrowing the candidate.
5. Compare evidence and choose one product for a sample trial.

## Decision principle

Do not select a product merely because it appears to have few suppliers. Select a clearly specified product with proven repeat demand, reachable buyers, controllable quality, reliable supply, and viable economics. Validate before investing.
